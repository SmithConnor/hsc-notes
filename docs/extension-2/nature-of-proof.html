The nature of proof

NoteSyllabus outcomes

ME2-12-01 selects and applies the language, notation and methods of proof to prove results.

MAO-WM-01 (Working mathematically) develops understanding and fluency in mathematics through exploring and connecting mathematical concepts, choosing and applying mathematical techniques to solve problems, and communicating their thinking and reasoning coherently and clearly.

Proof is the activity that separates mathematics from every other subject. A result is not accepted because it looks true, or because it works in every case we have tried, but because it can be deduced with certainty from things already established. This focus area builds the language and machinery of proof, so the rest of Extension 2 can be written and read precisely.

The language and notation of proof

A statement (or proposition) is a sentence that is either true or false, but not both. “\(7\) is prime” is a statement (true). “\(x > 3\)” on its own is not, because its truth depends on \(x\).

Most of proof is built from combining and transforming statements. The key vocabulary is below.

Connectives, negation and quantifiers

For statements \(P\) and \(Q\):

  1. The conjunction “\(P\) and \(Q\)” is written \(P \wedge Q\), and is true only when both \(P\) and \(Q\) are true.

  2. The disjunction “\(P\) or \(Q\)” is written \(P \vee Q\), and is true when at least one of \(P\), \(Q\) is true (this is the inclusive “or”).

  3. The negation of \(P\) is “not \(P\)”, written \(\neg P\) or \(\sim P\). It is true exactly when \(P\) is false.

  4. An implication is an “if–then” statement. “If \(P\) then \(Q\)” is written \(P \Rightarrow Q\) (or \(P \to Q\)) and read “\(P\) implies \(Q\)”. Here \(P\) is the hypothesis and \(Q\) is the conclusion.

The quantifiers let us make statements about many objects at once. “For all” is written \(\forall\), and “there exists” is written \(\exists\). For example, \(\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\ x^2 \geq 0\) reads “for all real \(x\), \(x^2\) is greater than or equal to zero”.

Negating compound statements

Negation interacts with connectives, quantifiers and implications in fixed ways. These are worth knowing cold, since many proofs begin by negating a statement.

  1. Double negation: \(\neg(\neg P) = P\).

  2. Negation of a conjunction: \(\neg(P \wedge Q) = (\neg P \vee \neg Q)\).

  3. Negation of a disjunction: \(\neg(P \vee Q) = (\neg P \wedge \neg Q)\).

  4. Negation of an implication: \(\neg(P \Rightarrow Q) = (P \wedge \neg Q)\).

The last one deserves attention. To deny “if \(P\) then \(Q\)”, you assert that \(P\) holds but \(Q\) fails. This also gives a useful rewriting of an implication itself:

\[P \Rightarrow Q \;=\; \neg(P \wedge \neg Q) \;=\; (\neg P \vee Q).\]

Negation also flips quantifiers: \(\neg(\forall x,\ P(x)) = \exists x,\ \neg P(x)\), and \(\neg(\exists x,\ P(x)) = \forall x,\ \neg P(x)\). To deny that something holds for all \(x\), produce a single \(x\) where it fails.

Converse, equivalence and contrapositive

Given the implication “if \(P\) then \(Q\)”, three related statements arise.

The converse is “if \(Q\) then \(P\)”, written \(Q \Rightarrow P\). The converse of a true implication may or may not be true. For example, “if \(n\) is a multiple of \(4\) then \(n\) is even” is true, but its converse “if \(n\) is even then \(n\) is a multiple of \(4\)” is false (\(n = 6\)).

Equivalence holds when both \(P \Rightarrow Q\) and \(Q \Rightarrow P\) are true. We write \(P \Leftrightarrow Q\) (or \(P \leftrightarrow Q\)), read “\(P\) if and only if \(Q\)”, commonly abbreviated “\(P\) iff \(Q\)”.

The contrapositive of “if \(P\) then \(Q\)” is “if not \(Q\) then not \(P\)”, written \(\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P\). An implication is always equivalent to its contrapositive:

\[(P \Rightarrow Q) \Leftrightarrow (\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P).\]

This equivalence is a genuine proof tool: when a direct proof of \(P \Rightarrow Q\) is awkward, proving the contrapositive instead is often easier and is just as valid.

Worked example: proof by contrapositive

Prove that for an integer \(n\), if \(n^2\) is even then \(n\) is even.

A direct attack (starting from “\(n^2\) is even”) is clumsy. The contrapositive is “if \(n\) is odd then \(n^2\) is odd”, which is easy.

Suppose \(n\) is odd, so \(n = 2k + 1\) for some integer \(k\). Then

\[n^2 = (2k+1)^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1,\]

which is odd. Since the contrapositive is true, the original statement is true. \(\blacksquare\)

Illustrations of proofs

Proof by contradiction

To prove a statement by contradiction, assume it is false, then derive a logical impossibility. Since the assumption led to something that cannot be, the assumption must have been wrong, so the original statement is true.

Worked example: irrationality of \(\sqrt{2}\)

Prove that \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational.

Assume the opposite: that \(\sqrt{2}\) is rational. Then \(\sqrt{2} = \dfrac{a}{b}\) for integers \(a, b\) with \(b \neq 0\) and the fraction in lowest terms (so \(a\) and \(b\) have no common factor).

Squaring gives \(2 = \dfrac{a^2}{b^2}\), so \(a^2 = 2b^2\). Then \(a^2\) is even, and by the earlier result \(a\) is even, say \(a = 2m\). Substituting,

\[(2m)^2 = 2b^2 \;\Rightarrow\; 4m^2 = 2b^2 \;\Rightarrow\; b^2 = 2m^2,\]

so \(b^2\) is even and hence \(b\) is even. But then \(a\) and \(b\) share the factor \(2\), contradicting the assumption that the fraction was in lowest terms. The assumption is therefore false, and \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational. \(\blacksquare\)

Examples and counterexamples

A single counterexample disproves a universal statement, because to deny “\(\forall x,\ P(x)\)” we need only one \(x\) with \(\neg P(x)\).

For instance, the claim “every odd number is prime” is false: \(9\) is odd but not prime. One counterexample settles it. Conversely, listing many examples where a statement holds never proves it; it only fails to disprove it.

Proofs involving integers

Many introductory proofs concern the integers, using the fact that any integer is either even (\(n = 2k\)) or odd (\(n = 2k + 1\)).

Worked example

Prove that the product of two consecutive integers is always even.

Let the integers be \(n\) and \(n + 1\). Consider two cases.

If \(n\) is even, \(n = 2k\), then \(n(n+1) = 2k(n+1)\), which is even.

If \(n\) is odd, \(n = 2k + 1\), then \(n + 1 = 2k + 2 = 2(k+1)\) is even, so \(n(n+1) = n \cdot 2(k+1)\) is even.

In both cases the product is even. \(\blacksquare\)

Proof of inequalities

Inequality proofs rely on a small toolkit of definitions and properties.

Foundational definitions and properties

The defining fact is: for real \(a\) and \(b\), \(a > b\) if and only if \(a - b > 0\). Reducing an inequality to “something positive” is the core move.

The other essential fact is that squares of real numbers are non-negative:

\[(a \pm b)^2 \geq 0 \quad \text{for all real } a, b.\]

The following results for real numbers are used freely once established: if \(a > b\) then \(a \pm c > b \pm c\); if \(a > b > 0\) then \(\dfrac{1}{b} > \dfrac{1}{a} > 0\) and conversely; if \(|a| > |b|\) then \(a^2 > b^2\) and conversely; if \(a > b\) and \(b > c\) then \(a > c\); if \(a > b\) and \(c > d\) then \(a + c > b + d\); if \(a > b\) and \(c > 0\) then \(ac > bc\); and if \(a > b\) and \(c < 0\) then \(ac < bc\) (multiplying by a negative reverses the inequality).

Worked example: using \((a-b)^2 \geq 0\)

Prove that \(a^2 + b^2 \geq 2ab\) for all real \(a\) and \(b\).

Since a square is never negative,

\[(a - b)^2 \geq 0 \;\Rightarrow\; a^2 - 2ab + b^2 \geq 0 \;\Rightarrow\; a^2 + b^2 \geq 2ab. \qquad \blacksquare\]

Equality holds exactly when \(a = b\).

The triangle inequality

For real numbers \(a\) and \(b\),

\[|a + b| \leq |a| + |b|.\]

Geometrically, the direct distance is never longer than a detour: the length of \(a + b\) cannot exceed the combined lengths of \(a\) and \(b\). Equality holds when \(a\) and \(b\) have the same sign (or one is zero).

The arithmetic mean – geometric mean (AM–GM) inequality

For two non-negative numbers, the arithmetic mean is at least the geometric mean:

\[\forall a, b \geq 0, \quad \frac{a + b}{2} \geq \sqrt{ab}.\]

This follows directly from a square being non-negative. Starting from \(\left(\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b}\right)^2 \geq 0\),

\[a - 2\sqrt{ab} + b \geq 0 \;\Rightarrow\; a + b \geq 2\sqrt{ab} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{a+b}{2} \geq \sqrt{ab},\]

with equality when \(a = b\).

The squeeze theorem

If \(f(x) \leq g(x) \leq h(x)\) for all \(x\) near \(k\) (though not necessarily at \(k\)), and

\[\lim_{x \to k} f(x) = \lim_{x \to k} h(x) = L,\]

then \(\lim_{x \to k} g(x) = L\). If the outer functions are squeezed to the same limit, the trapped function has nowhere else to go.

Strategies for proving inequalities

Beyond the specific results above, inequalities can be proved by building on previously established inequalities, by geometric arguments, or by graphical and calculus techniques (such as showing a function is always positive by examining its minimum), or a combination of these.

Further proof by mathematical induction

Mathematical induction proves that a statement holds for all integers from some starting point. It has two parts: a base case, where the statement is verified for the first value, and an inductive step, where you assume the statement for some value and prove it for the next.

A recursive formula (or recurrence relation) defines each term of a sequence using a preceding term, for example \(a_{n+1} = 2a_n + 1\). Induction is the natural tool for proving closed-form results about such sequences.

Worked example: induction with an inequality

Prove that \(2^n > n^2\) for all integers \(n \geq 5\).

Base case (\(n = 5\)): \(2^5 = 32\) and \(5^2 = 25\), and \(32 > 25\), so the statement holds.

Inductive step: assume \(2^k > k^2\) for some integer \(k \geq 5\). We must show \(2^{k+1} > (k+1)^2\).

\[2^{k+1} = 2 \cdot 2^k > 2k^2 \quad \text{(by the assumption).}\]

It now suffices to show \(2k^2 \geq (k+1)^2\) for \(k \geq 5\). Expanding, \(2k^2 - (k+1)^2 = k^2 - 2k - 1\), which for \(k \geq 5\) is \(k^2 - 2k - 1 = k(k-2) - 1 \geq 5(3) - 1 = 14 > 0\). Hence \(2k^2 > (k+1)^2\), and so

\[2^{k+1} > 2k^2 > (k+1)^2.\]

By induction, \(2^n > n^2\) for all integers \(n \geq 5\). \(\blacksquare\)
